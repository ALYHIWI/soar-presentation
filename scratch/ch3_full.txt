Chapter 3:

Proposed System Architecture

 

3.1	Introduction

This chapter presents the proposed AI-Based Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) tool and describes the architecture through which security alerts are collected, enriched, analyzed, prioritized, and connected to controlled response actions. The proposed tool combines conventional SOAR capabilities with AI/ML-based analysis to support context-aware alert assessment, dynamic prioritization, and policy-controlled incident response.

The architecture follows a modular design in which each component performs a distinct operational function. Security alerts are first collected and normalized through the ingestion layer, after which

contextual information is added to improve alert interpretation. The enriched alerts are then processed by the machine-learning components to produce contextual risk information that supports prioritization,

suppression, and response-policy decisions. High-impact response actions remain subject to predefined policy controls and human approval where necessary.

In addition to the functional modules, the proposed tool incorporates an agent-oriented architecture

consisting primarily of an Attacker Agent and a Defender Agent, informed by the AI-driven Multi-Agent System approach presented by Agrawal et al. (2026). The Attacker Agent supports adaptive attack

simulation within the controlled testing environment, whereas the Defender Agent coordinates intelligent

defensive analysis and decision support. Detailed implementation, training, and evaluation of the agents are deferred to Chapter 4.

The following sections describe the high-level architecture, agent layer, ingestion and enrichment components, machine-learning triage and scoring engine, prioritization logic, automated response policy, and analyst feedback mechanism.

3.2	High-Level Architecture Overview

The proposed AI-Based Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) tool is designed as a modular pipeline that connects security-alert collection, contextual analysis, machine-learning-based risk assessment, dynamic prioritization, and controlled incident-response execution. The architecture builds on the complementary roles of SOAR and AI/ML identified in the literature: SOAR provides workflow

orchestration, integration, and execution capabilities, while AI/ML can support data-driven analysis,

classification, prioritization, and decision support (Khayat et al., 2025; Kinyua & Awuah, 2021). Rather than replacing conventional SOAR functions, the proposed tool introduces an intelligent decision layer between alert collection and response execution.

In addition to its functional organization, the proposed AI-Based SOAR tool adopts an agent-oriented architecture inspired by recent AI-driven multi-agent cybersecurity research. Two principal agent roles are considered: an Attacker Agent, which operates within the controlled testing environment to generate

adaptive attack activity, and a Defender Agent, which supports intelligent alert analysis, contextual risk assessment, prioritization, and response decision making. The agent layer does not replace the functional modules of the architecture; rather, it provides an intelligent coordination layer through which selected analytical and decision-making functions can be performed. The detailed implementation, training

procedures, learning algorithms, and interaction mechanisms of these agents are presented in Chapter 4 (Agrawal et al., 2026).

At a high level, the architecture begins with security data sources, which may include SIEM platforms, intrusion-detection systems, endpoint-security technologies, network-security controls, and other monitoring tools. Alerts generated by these sources are received through the Ingestion and Connectors Module, which provides a common entry point for heterogeneous security data. The module is responsible

 

for collecting and normalizing incoming alert information into a consistent representation that can be processed by the subsequent components of the tool.

The normalized alert is then passed to the Context Enrichment Layer. This layer supplements the original alert with additional information relevant to its security significance. Depending on the available

environment and data sources, enrichment may include threat-intelligence indicators, asset criticality, user or identity information, source reputation, and relationships with recent or historical security events. The

enriched alert is subsequently provided to the Machine Learning Triage and Scoring Engine, which

analyzes the available features and produces a contextual risk assessment. The principal output of this stage is a risk score representing the estimated importance of the alert, together with any additional model output required by the prioritization process.

The resulting score is processed by the Prioritization and Suppression Logic, which determines how incoming alerts should be ordered and whether selected low-risk or redundant alerts are eligible for

suppression or deprioritization according to predefined policy conditions. This stage converts model output into an operational priority that can be used to allocate analyst attention more effectively. High-risk alerts are therefore intended to receive greater operational attention than alerts assessed as low risk, while

suppression decisions remain governed by configurable rules rather than by the machine-learning model alone.

After prioritization, the alert is passed to the Automated Response Policy. This component determines the appropriate level of response based on factors such as contextual risk, alert priority,

confidence, and the potential operational impact of the available action. The proposed architecture separates intelligent analysis from response execution so that high-impact actions are not triggered directly by an

unverified model prediction. Depending on the policy decision, an alert may be suppressed, monitored, escalated for analyst investigation, submitted for human approval, or forwarded to a SOAR workflow for automated execution. This controlled approach is consistent with human-AI teaming research that

emphasizes flexible autonomy and human involvement in uncertain or high-risk situations (Chhetri et al., 2024).

Finally, the Analyst Feedback Loop records analyst decisions and investigation outcomes. Feedback may include confirmation or correction of alert priority, validation of the risk assessment, approval or

rejection of recommended actions, and the final incident disposition. This information can be retained for subsequent analysis, threshold adjustment, and future model refinement rather than assuming unrestricted online learning during live operation.

3.2.1	Agent Layer Overview

The proposed AI-Based SOAR tool incorporates an agent layer to support adaptive attack simulation and intelligent defensive decision making. This design is informed by the AI-driven Multi-Agent System (MAS) presented by Agrawal et al. (2026), which employs attacker and defender agents within a controlled cyber-range environment. In the reference system, reinforcement-learning techniques are used to support adaptive attacker behavior, while machine-learning-based defender agents perform threat detection and

support incident-response decisions.

Within the proposed tool, the Attacker Agent is primarily associated with the testing and evaluation environment rather than with the operational SOAR workflow itself. Its purpose is to generate adaptive or multi-stage attack activity that produces security events and alerts for evaluating the defensive components of the system. The Attacker Agent can interact with the controlled environment and modify its behavior according to environmental state and observed defensive responses. The detailed state representation,

available actions, reward mechanism, and reinforcement-learning approach are deferred to Chapter 4.

The Defender Agent represents the principal intelligent defensive role within the proposed

architecture. It receives security information produced by the ingestion and contextual-enrichment stages and interacts with the machine-learning components responsible for risk assessment, classification where applicable, and alert prioritization. The Defender Agent also uses the resulting analytical information to

support response-policy evaluation. This role is consistent with Agrawal et al. (2026), where defender agents employ machine-learning techniques for threat detection and participate in automated incident-response

decision making.

The agent layer is therefore integrated with, rather than separated from, the functional architecture.

The Attacker Agent provides adaptive security activity within the controlled testing environment, while the Defender Agent coordinates intelligent defensive analysis and decision support before operational actions are executed through SOAR workflows and applicable human-approval controls.

 

Chapter 3 defines the architectural responsibilities and placement of these agents, while Chapter 4 describes their implementation, machine-learning and reinforcement-learning techniques, training

procedures, interaction mechanisms, and experimental evaluation.

The following sections describe each architectural component and its role within the proposed workflow in greater detail.

3.3	Ingestion and Connectors Module

The Ingestion and Connectors Module serves as the entry point of the proposed AI-Based SOAR tool. Its primary purpose is to receive security alerts and associated telemetry from heterogeneous security technologies and transform them into a consistent representation that can be processed by the downstream analytical components. Modern SOC environments commonly combine multiple products with different

data formats, interfaces, and representations, which can complicate unified security analysis and

orchestration (Kinyua & Awuah, 2021). Accordingly, the proposed module is designed to separate source-specific integration concerns from the internal processing logic of the tool.

Security information may originate from different categories of technologies, including SIEM platforms, intrusion-detection systems, endpoint-security solutions, network-security controls, and other monitoring or detection mechanisms. Depending on the implementation environment, these sources may expose alerts through REST APIs, webhooks, message streams, log files, or other supported interfaces. Rather than requiring the remaining components of the architecture to understand each vendor-specific format, individual connectors are responsible for communicating with the corresponding source and

translating incoming data into a common internal alert structure.

The normalization process preserves the security-relevant information contained in the original event while standardizing fields required by the proposed tool. A normalized alert may contain attributes such as an alert identifier, timestamp, source system, alert type, original severity, source and destination information, affected user or asset, event description, and any available indicators of compromise. The

module should also retain a reference to the original alert or raw event data when available so that normalized information can be traced back to its source during investigation or validation.

Before forwarding an alert to subsequent components, the module performs basic validation and

preprocessing. This may include verifying required fields, converting timestamps into a consistent format, standardizing field names and categorical values, removing malformed or incomplete records where appropriate, and identifying duplicate events according to predefined criteria. Such operations are intended to improve data consistency rather than to determine whether an alert is malicious or important. Risk

assessment and prioritization remain the responsibility of later modules.

The use of connector-based integration also supports interoperability with multi-vendor security environments. SOAR platforms depend on integrations with external technologies to query information and initiate operational actions, while differences among vendor interfaces can create substantial integration effort (Bridges et al., 2023; Sworna et al., 2023). In the proposed architecture, this problem is addressed through an abstraction layer in which each connector implements the communication requirements of a

particular data source while exposing a standardized output to the rest of the tool. New sources can therefore be incorporated by developing or configuring an additional connector without redesigning the machine-

learning or prioritization components.





Each normalized record should also maintain source provenance. Information such as the originating security product, connector identifier, ingestion timestamp, and original alert identifier can be retained to

 

support traceability and later response decisions. This is particularly important when automated or analyst-approved actions must be related back to the evidence that triggered the workflow.

The output of the Ingestion and Connectors Module is therefore a validated and normalized alert

object that provides a common foundation for further processing. The normalized alert is forwarded to the Context Enrichment Layer, where additional information from internal and external sources is attached before machine-learning-based risk assessment is performed.

3.4	Context Enrichment Layer

The Context Enrichment Layer is responsible for extending the normalized alert with additional information that can improve its interpretation before machine-learning-based risk assessment is performed. An alert considered in isolation may provide only limited information about its operational significance,

whereas surrounding behavioral, organizational, and threat-intelligence context can help distinguish routine activity from events that warrant greater attention. Context-aware analysis has been shown to improve

security-event triage by incorporating relationships and surrounding activity rather than relying solely on individual alert attributes (Liu et al., 2022).

The proposed layer receives the normalized alert produced by the Ingestion and Connectors Module and enriches it using information available from internal and external security sources. The enrichment

process is modular so that the tool can operate with different combinations of contextual information

depending on the deployment environment. Potential enrichment categories include asset context, identity context, threat-intelligence context, historical context, and behavioral or temporal context.

Asset context provides information about the system or resource associated with an alert. Relevant attributes may include asset type, business criticality, network location, operating environment, or known exposure information. For example, an alert affecting a critical production server may require different

operational treatment from a similar alert affecting a low-criticality test system. Asset information therefore provides organizational context that cannot necessarily be inferred from the original security alert alone.

 

Identity context may include information related to the user, account, or service associated with an event. Depending on data availability, this may include account type, privilege level, authentication history, or whether the identity is associated with a sensitive resource. Such information can help distinguish events involving ordinary accounts from those involving privileged or otherwise high-impact identities.

Threat-intelligence context enriches observable indicators contained in an alert, such as IP addresses, domains, URLs, or file hashes, with information obtained from available reputation or threat-intelligence

sources. SOAR environments commonly integrate threat-intelligence services to support investigation and contextualization of incoming alerts (Bridges et al., 2023). In the proposed tool, this information may

include reputation status, indicator type, known malicious associations, or other metadata returned by the available intelligence source.

The layer may also incorporate historical and behavioral context. Historical information can indicate whether similar alerts, indicators, users, or assets have appeared previously, while temporal relationships can identify activity occurring within a relevant time window. This is important because the significance of an event can depend on surrounding behavior. Context2Vector, for example, demonstrates that similar security events can have different operational interpretations depending on the behavioral context in which they

occur (Liu et al., 2022). The proposed architecture therefore allows recent event history and related activity to contribute contextual information before scoring.

The enrichment process does not itself assign the final alert priority or determine the response action. Its purpose is to create an enriched alert representation containing both the original normalized attributes and the contextual information required by subsequent analysis. Each added attribute should retain its source and availability status where practical so that the system can distinguish between verified contextual information, missing values, and unavailable enrichment sources.

Because external or internal context may occasionally be unavailable, the architecture should not assume that every alert will contain every enrichment field. Missing contextual information can be

represented explicitly and handled during later feature preparation and model development. This design allows the tool to degrade gracefully when a particular enrichment service or organizational data source is unavailable instead of preventing the alert from proceeding through the pipeline.

The output of the Context Enrichment Layer is an enriched alert object that is forwarded to the

Machine Learning Triage and Scoring Engine. The subsequent engine transforms the available alert and contextual attributes into the features required for machine-learning-based analysis and produces the risk information used by the prioritization component.

3.5	Machine Learning Triage and Scoring Engine

The Machine Learning Triage and Scoring Engine is the analytical core of the proposed AI-Based SOAR tool. Its purpose is to evaluate each enriched alert using both its original security attributes and the contextual information supplied by the Context Enrichment Layer. Rather than relying exclusively on static severity values or manually defined thresholds, the engine applies a trained machine-learning model to

estimate the operational risk associated with an alert and provide structured information to the subsequent prioritization component.

From the agent-oriented perspective, the Machine Learning Triage and Scoring Engine represents a principal analytical capability of the Defender Agent. The agent receives the enriched alert representation, invokes the selected machine-learning models, and uses their outputs to support contextual risk assessment and subsequent prioritization. The Defender Agent therefore coordinates the analytical process, while the

ML engine remains responsible for producing the model-derived risk, classification, and confidence-related outputs defined by the architecture.

The engine receives an enriched alert and transforms the available attributes into a model-ready

feature representation. Depending on the selected model and dataset, these features may include properties derived from the original alert, asset and identity characteristics, threat-intelligence indicators, historical

observations, and behavioral or temporal context. Context-aware security research has shown that

incorporating surrounding activity can improve the interpretation and prioritization of alerts compared with evaluating isolated event attributes alone (Liu et al., 2022). Similarly, feedback-driven approaches such as AlertPro demonstrate the value of combining alert properties with contextual and historical features for alert ranking (Wang et al., 2024).

The primary output of the engine is a contextual risk score representing the estimated operational

significance of the alert. For architectural purposes, the score can be normalized to a consistent range, such as 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater estimated risk. The exact mathematical interpretation of this score depends on the machine-learning technique selected during model development. For example, it may

 

represent a predicted probability, anomaly-related score, or another model-derived measure that is

subsequently normalized for operational use. The detailed model-selection, training, feature-engineering, and score-calibration procedures are described in Chapter 4.

In addition to the primary risk score, the engine may produce a predicted class or actionability label when supported by the selected model. Such a label may distinguish, for example, between alerts estimated to require further investigation and those considered lower priority. Previous SOC research has

demonstrated the feasibility of learning patterns associated with historical analyst handling decisions through supervised classification (Gupta et al., 2019). However, the proposed architecture does not rely on a binary classification alone; the continuous risk score remains the principal input to dynamic prioritization

because it provides a finer basis for ordering alerts with different estimated levels of importance.

A confidence indicator may also be retained when it can be derived reliably from the selected model. This value is conceptually distinct from the risk score. Risk represents the estimated security significance of an alert, whereas confidence reflects the certainty associated with the model's assessment. Maintaining this distinction is particularly important for controlled automation. A high-risk prediction accompanied by low confidence may require analyst validation, while a high-confidence assessment may support a different

response-policy decision. The exact method used to estimate or calibrate confidence is deferred to the model-development stage and will depend on the final algorithm.

Where supported by the selected technique, the engine may additionally expose explanatory information such as the features that contributed most strongly to the resulting score. Such information can support analyst interpretation and later validation, but explainability is treated as a supporting output rather than as a substitute for the risk assessment itself.

The engine is deliberately separated from both prioritization and response execution. It does not directly suppress alerts, reorder the analyst queue, or initiate containment actions. Instead, it produces analytical outputs that are consumed by later architectural components. This separation prevents a raw

machine-learning prediction from being translated directly into a potentially high-impact response without additional policy checks.

The contextual risk score is the mandatory analytical output of the proposed design, while

classification and confidence are supporting outputs whose implementation depends on the final machine-

learning model. This design keeps the architecture sufficiently flexible to accommodate different algorithms while preserving a consistent interface with the subsequent prioritization component.

The Prioritization and Suppression Logic converts the analytical outputs of the Machine Learning Triage and Scoring Engine into operational decisions about alert ordering and analyst attention. While the ML engine estimates the contextual risk associated with each alert, this module determines how that score should influence the active investigation queue and whether selected alerts are eligible for suppression or

deprioritization. Separating these responsibilities allows model predictions to be interpreted through explicit operational policies rather than being applied directly to incident handling.

Within the agent layer, the Defender Agent uses the outputs of the ML Triage and Scoring Engine as inputs to the prioritization process. The agent does not independently discard alerts; suppression and

deprioritization remain governed by the explicit policy conditions and safeguards defined by this module.

The principal input to this module is the contextual risk score produced by the ML engine.

Supporting information, such as the model confidence indicator, original alert severity, asset criticality, or other policy-relevant attributes, may also be considered where available. The resulting operational priority can be represented through ordered categories such as Low, Medium, High, and Critical, or through a

continuously ranked queue. The exact thresholds separating these categories are configurable and should be established during system evaluation rather than assumed to be universally applicable across SOC environments.

Dynamic prioritization is intended to ensure that an alert's position is determined by its current

contextual assessment rather than solely by the static severity assigned by the originating security product.

As new alerts enter the system or additional context becomes available, their relative positions can be

recalculated according to the applicable prioritization policy. This design is consistent with prior research

demonstrating that contextual information and analyst feedback can improve alert ranking beyond an initial anomaly or severity score (Wang et al., 2024).

The module also includes suppression and deprioritization logic for alerts that satisfy predefined low-risk conditions. Suppression is intended to reduce unnecessary analyst workload by preventing selected alerts from repeatedly occupying investigation capacity. However, the proposed architecture does not allow the machine-learning score alone to permanently discard an alert. A suppression decision should depend on explicit policy conditions, which may include a sufficiently low contextual risk score, adequate model

confidence, duplicate or repeated-event status, absence of high-criticality assets or identities, and the lack of other escalation conditions. Machine-learning-based alert scoring has previously been explored for false-

positive suppression and queue management, illustrating the potential operational value of combining scoring with controlled filtering mechanisms (Gelman et al., 2023).

To reduce the risk of hiding significant incidents, the module distinguishes between suppression and deprioritization. Deprioritized alerts remain available for later inspection but are placed below higher-risk events in the investigation queue. Suppressed alerts are excluded from the primary queue according to the active policy but should remain recorded for audit, retrospective analysis, and possible reconsideration if

related evidence emerges. This distinction preserves traceability while reducing immediate analyst workload.

The prioritization logic also supports policy overrides. Certain alerts may be prevented from

suppression regardless of their model score when they involve critical assets, privileged identities, known high-risk indicators, or other organization-defined conditions. Similarly, low-confidence predictions can be

 

routed for analyst review instead of being automatically suppressed. These safeguards ensure that operational rules remain capable of constraining the influence of the ML model when the potential consequence of an incorrect prioritization decision is high.

The main output of this module is therefore an operationally prioritized alert associated with a priority level and a handling status. The handling status may indicate that the alert should remain in the investigation queue, be deprioritized, be suppressed according to policy, or be forwarded for additional

response evaluation. The module does not itself execute containment or remediation actions; alerts requiring further action are passed to the Automated Response Policy described in the next section.

3.7	Automated Response Policy

The Automated Response Policy determines how a prioritized alert should be handled after its contextual risk and operational priority have been established. Its purpose is to translate analytical and

prioritization outputs into a controlled response decision while preventing high-impact actions from being triggered directly by a machine-learning prediction. This module therefore acts as a policy boundary

between intelligent alert assessment and SOAR-based execution.

From the agent perspective, the Defender Agent uses the outputs of the analytical and prioritization components to support response-policy evaluation. However, the agent does not bypass the control

mechanisms of the SOAR architecture. High-impact actions remain subject to predefined policy constraints and, where required, explicit human approval before SOAR-based execution.

The policy receives information from the preceding modules, including the contextual risk score, assigned priority level, model confidence where available, alert handling status, and relevant contextual

attributes such as asset criticality, identity sensitivity, threat-intelligence findings, and the type of response action under consideration. These inputs are evaluated against predefined response rules to determine the appropriate level of automation.

 

The proposed tool distinguishes among several response outcomes. A low-risk alert may require no immediate response beyond continued monitoring or recording. Alerts requiring investigation may be

escalated to an analyst without triggering automated containment. Where a response action is appropriate but may have significant operational consequences, the policy can require human approval before the corresponding SOAR workflow is executed. Lower-risk and well-defined actions may be eligible for

automated execution when the applicable policy conditions are satisfied.

The decision to automate an action should therefore consider not only the estimated security risk of the alert but also the potential impact of the response itself. For example, performing an enrichment query or

creating an incident ticket generally carries less operational risk than disabling a privileged account,

isolating a production endpoint, or modifying a firewall policy. This distinction supports a risk-aware level of autonomy, in which the amount of human involvement depends on the uncertainty and consequence

associated with the decision. Human-AI teaming research similarly emphasizes flexible autonomy and selective deferral when confidence is low or operational risk is high (Chhetri et al., 2024).

For architectural purposes, response handling can be represented through the following policy states:

•	Monitor / Record: retain the alert and associated evidence without initiating a containment action.

•	Analyst Investigation: forward the alert to the analyst queue for further validation.

•	Human Approval Required: generate a recommended response but require explicit analyst authorization before execution.

•	Automated Response: execute an approved low-risk or well-defined response workflow when the policy conditions are satisfied.

The exact thresholds and rules used to select among these states are configurable and should be

determined during implementation and evaluation. A critical alert does not automatically imply unrestricted automated response. For example, a high-risk alert affecting a critical production asset may instead require mandatory human approval because the consequence of an incorrect containment action could be

substantial. Similarly, low model confidence can override otherwise eligible automation and route the case to analyst review.

Once a response decision has been made, the module selects or invokes the appropriate SOAR

workflow or playbook. The workflow may perform actions such as threat-intelligence enrichment, ticket creation, notification, blocking of a malicious indicator, account-related actions, endpoint containment, or other security operations supported by the connected technologies. SOAR playbooks provide a mechanism for coordinating defensive actions across multiple security controls, while APIs enable these workflows to interact with external security products (Karlzén & Sommestad, 2023; Sworna et al., 2023).

All policy decisions and response actions should be logged for traceability. The recorded information may include the alert identifier, risk score, priority, selected policy state, whether human approval was

required, the executed workflow, action outcome, and relevant timestamps. This audit trail allows analysts to review why a response was selected and provides information that can later contribute to system evaluation and the Analyst Feedback Loop.

3.8	Analyst Feedback Loop

The Analyst Feedback Loop provides a mechanism for incorporating human validation and

investigation outcomes into the proposed AI-Based SOAR tool. Its purpose is not to allow the system to modify its behavior autonomously during live operation, but to capture analyst judgments and response

outcomes so that model performance, prioritization policies, and response rules can be evaluated and refined over time.

Feedback may be generated at several points in the workflow. During alert investigation, an analyst may confirm or reject the model's risk assessment, adjust the assigned priority, identify whether the alert represents a true or false positive, or provide a final incident disposition. During response handling, the analyst may approve or reject a recommended action, select an alternative response, or record whether an executed workflow achieved the intended result. These decisions provide operational evidence about the quality of both the analytical and response components of the system.

The proposed tool records feedback together with the alert information that produced the original

decision. A feedback record may include the alert identifier, contextual risk score, assigned priority, model confidence where available, analyst-adjusted priority, final incident classification, selected response action, approval or rejection decision, response outcome, and relevant timestamps. Maintaining this linkage allows later analysis to compare machine-generated recommendations with human decisions and observed

operational results.

Analyst feedback can support several improvement activities. First, it can be used to assess whether the machine-learning model consistently overestimates or underestimates risk for particular types of alerts.

Second, accumulated analyst decisions can provide additional labeled examples for periodic model

retraining or validation. Third, feedback can support adjustment of prioritization thresholds and suppression policies when operational results indicate that the current configuration produces excessive false positives, unnecessary escalations, or missed high-priority events. Feedback-driven alert prioritization has been

 

explored in previous research, where analyst validation is used to influence subsequent alert rankings (Wang et al., 2024).

The feedback mechanism also supports the controlled-autonomy principle adopted by the proposed architecture. Human-AI teaming research emphasizes that analysts and AI systems should be able to share information and adjust decision authority according to the situation, particularly when uncertainty or

operational risk is high (Chhetri et al., 2024). In the proposed tool, analyst intervention therefore serves both an immediate operational role and a longer-term learning role by identifying cases in which automated

assessment or policy behavior requires refinement.

The architecture distinguishes between operational feedback and model updating. Operational

feedback is collected continuously as analysts interact with alerts and response recommendations. Model

retraining, threshold modification, or policy adjustment, however, should occur through a controlled offline process after sufficient feedback has been reviewed and validated. This separation prevents unverified

analyst inputs or isolated decisions from immediately altering the behavior of the production model.

The collected analyst feedback can also support the periodic offline evaluation and refinement of the decision models associated with the Defender Agent. This refinement remains a controlled process and

does not imply unrestricted online modification of agent behavior during live operation.

All feedback should remain traceable to the corresponding alert and response workflow. This creates an auditable record that can support performance evaluation, error analysis, and future model development. Over time, the accumulated feedback can help identify recurring disagreement between the AI/ML engine and analysts, assess the suitability of automation policies, and guide revisions to the tool.
